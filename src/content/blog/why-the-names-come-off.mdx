There is a version of the NBA draft where you are handed a spreadsheet and nothing
else. No school, no highlight reel, no name, no mock draft consensus that has been
sitting in your head since October. Just production, measurements, and how good the
team around them actually was.

Almost nobody wants to draft that way, and the reason is reasonable: the deleted
information is not noise. Where someone played matters. Who they played against
matters. The eye test is a real instrument.

But it is very hard to tell, from the inside, how much of your read on a prospect is
coming from the numbers and how much is coming from the fact that you have heard the
name four hundred times. The two feel identical while you're doing it. Confidence
doesn't come with a source attached.

## The only way to find out is to remove it

So that's the whole design. The board shows you everything except the identity, you
draft against the real order — players actually taken before your slot are gone, the
same way they were gone in real life — and at the end the names go back on.

What you get is not a score against some model of who *should* have been drafted
where. It's a comparison against what a room full of professionals with far more
information than you actually did. Sometimes you beat them. That result is more
interesting than it sounds, because you were working with a strict subset of what
they had.

## It generalizes further than basketball

The same structure works anywhere reputation arrives before evidence, which is most
places. Film financing is the obvious next one: two pitches, budget and genre and
release window and the shape of the deal, no titles and no cast. Pick the one you'd
have funded. Then the box office opens.

The pattern is always the same three steps — hide the thing everyone is really
judging on, force a commitment, then reveal. The commitment is the load-bearing
part. Reading about a bias is free. Getting it wrong on the record, with your own
pick sitting there in front of you, is not.

## What this can't tell you

A caveat worth stating up front, since it applies to every game here: college
production is not evenly measured. International prospects have thinner records.
Some eras have better tracking than others. A prospect who looks flat on the page
may have been playing a role that suppressed everything the page records.

That's not a bug to be fixed so much as the actual terrain — real scouting has the
same holes, and it's part of what makes the numbers-only version hard. But when a
pick looks indefensible in hindsight, it's worth checking whether the data ever had
what you'd have needed.
